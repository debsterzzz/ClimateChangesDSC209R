How Our Planet Changed in Two Decades

From 1992 to 2010, the planet warmed, seas rose, and climate-related disasters became more frequent. The scientific record is clear, but humanity's sentiment towards it was not.

For nearly two decades, the world argued about whether the climate was truly changing — not because the data was unclear, but because the change was uneven.

Global temperatures climbed, sea levels rose, and disasters became more common.
Some countries warmed dramatically while others cooled for a few years. Some regions were hit by repeated floods, storms, and heatwaves, while others saw almost nothing unusual. This unevenness made climate change harder to accept, especially in “quieter” places.
The phrase “global warming” was meant to raise awareness — but it ultimately became a double-edged sword. Skeptics pointed to cold winters or stable regions and claimed the whole idea was exaggerated, mistaking local weather for global climate.
To understand what really happened, we need to see both stories at once: the planet as a whole, and the specific country we care about most.

This explorable explanation lets you do exactly that. You can watch the world change over time, then zoom into a single country and ask: How does my local experience fit into the global pattern?

How to Explore the Map

Use the controls below to compare the world’s climate story with your own country’s experience. The goal isn’t just to see rising numbers, but to understand why climate change felt urgent in some places and distant in others.

  • Use the year slider to move through time.
  • Press Play to animate the years automatically.
  • Switch between Temperature, Sea Level, and Disasters using the mode buttons.
  • Learn about major climate related events through the Key Events Panel or by hovering over the Icons that appear on the map
  • Hover over any country to see exact values.
  • Use the Country Search feature to zoom into your home country and discover its climate story through sparkline graphs and a short summary.
  • The Global Snapshot Bar summarizes worldwide conditions for the selected year.
  • Switch between global temperature anomaly, sea level, and disaster count charts in the right panel while exploring in any mode. Easily discover any correlations between these metrics on a global scale or a selected country.
  • Click Reset to clear any selected country and reset to map to its default zoom.

Why These Events Matter

As you move through time, you’ll see key events appear on the map and in the Key Events panel on the right: volcanic eruptions, record heatwaves, major hurricanes, historic floods, and global climate negotiations. Each marker represents a moment when the climate story became impossible to ignore in a specific place.

Some events temporarily cooled the planet, like the aerosols from the 1991 Mount Pinatubo eruption. Others pushed temperatures to new records or unleashed devastating disasters in regions that had rarely seen such extremes before. Policy milestones — like the Rio Earth Summit or the Kyoto Protocol — capture how slowly our global response evolved compared to the pace of physical change.

These events help explain why climate change was so hard to recognize as a shared reality. For people living far from these disasters, or in regions that cooled for a few years, the crisis could feel distant or even exaggerated. By placing these events on top of the global data, the visualization shows how local shocks and long-term trends combine into one uneven, but unmistakable, planetary shift.

What Should You Take Away?

The main insight from this visualization is that climate change is not a single smooth line — it is thousands of regional stories unfolding at different speeds. Some countries warm twice as fast as the global average, others cool temporarily, and some see sharp jumps in disaster frequency while their neighbors remain relatively quiet.

For decades, this unevenness slowed global acceptance. People looked out their window, saw familiar seasons, and wondered how “global warming” could be real. The term itself became a rhetorical trap: cold winters and local cooling were used as proof that the science was wrong, rather than as natural variation layered on top of a clear long-term trend.

By letting you compare the planet’s overall trajectory with any single country, this visualization closes that gap between global evidence and local experience. When you see both stories side by side, the pattern becomes hard to dismiss: the world really did change between 1992 and 2010 — even if it didn’t always feel that way where you happened to live.

Climate change is no longer an abstract debate. It is a set of concrete histories written into the temperature, sea level, and disaster records of every country on Earth. The question moving forward is not whether it is happening, but how we choose to respond.
